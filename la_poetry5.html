The Cars

  • By Kiana Pray
  • Poetry

Kiana Pray is a rising junior at Crystal Springs Uplands High School in California, born and raised in the CA Bay Area. Her poetry has been published in the San Mateo Daily Journal, and she is an alumnus of the Kenyon Review Young Writers Summer Online Workshop. She has been writing for Crystal’s satire paper, the Gryphon Whiffer, since her freshman year, and she will be the Editor-In-Chief starting this year. In her free time, she enjoys collecting stationery and watching murder mysteries.

“The Cars” was inspired by our trip to the Brooklyn Promenade and Colson Whitehead’s essay, “Brooklyn Bridge.”

The Cars

Impolite city wind
tugs your hair in
every wrong direction.
It lifts leaves from
the cracked sidewalk,
pulling them into
a jagged, midair waltz.
Car horns sounded by
impatient hands
punctuate the
sluggish cadence of
downtown traffic.
Cars slide along
the horizon in a
steady rhythm,
blurry taxis and
anxious commuters
reduced to
shiny dots on the
distant pavement.
If you lean back on
your wooden bench
and let your eyes
unfocus, the vehicles
go fuzzy in your
waterline,
swallowed up by
gray scrapes of
offices and
high-rise apartments.
With a shift of your
gaze, the shapes
reveal themselves,
a sensation privy to
the more observant
onlookers.
You wonder about
the cars moving past,
whether their drivers
crank up the AC or
roll their windows down,
whether their speakers are
pulsing to the beat of
pop or alt rock.
You wonder if the sour scent
of a guilty cigarette
clings to the driver’s coat,
if their shampoo smells of
vanilla or rosemary.
As your eyes follow the
clockwork locomotion
of moving cars,
every slight discomfort
slowly falls away.
The sounds of the
city blur into
a negligible hum,
no longer oppressive
or wanting.
The midday sunlight
washes your skin
in warmth, but it’s
kinder now, gently
setting the fuzz on your
arms aglow.
The wet summer air
tucks itself into the
creases of
your elbows,
the spaces behind
your knees,
though the sensation
tugs at your mind
less than before.
Despite the stickiness on
your skin, a remnant
of the air’s moist breath;
despite the faint, yet
unmistakably rotten aroma
of left-out trash,
your body softly molds into
the shape of the bench
beneath you,
engraving pink shapes
into your skin,
while your
slow-blinking eyes
focus on the
comforting constance
of cars
driving past.
