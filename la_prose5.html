Selected Works by Ariette Bolle

  • By Ariette Bolle
  • Prose / Novel Excerpts

Ariette Bolle is a rising freshman at Wheaton College in Norton, Massachusetts. Born and raised in New York City, she now lives in Massachusetts. She spends her free time creating art, listening to and creating music, and undertaking her ever-growing movie watchlist.

I remember

I remember 105th street in the spring, looking up at a canopy of light green leaves and white blossoms. Down on the ground, in my stroller on the concrete, awed, the trees seemed so impossibly huge above me. I can still hear the sparrows chirping, an accompaniment to the soft scrape of the stroller’s wheels against the sidewalk.

I remember shivering on the preschool playground, trying to be subtle with my discomfort as I rubbed my arms beneath my thin sweater in the freezing winter air. I remember my refusal to wear the puffy, swampy green coat that my babysitter had kindly gifted to my sister and I when it didn’t fit her daughters anymore. (I thought it was hideous.) I remember the loathing I felt towards the teacher who ushered me inside and forced me to put it on, my insides twisting in a humiliated tangle.

I remember cookies with honey at Alice’s Teacup. Sitting beside my sister in our favorite booth, the one at the back of the restaurant, with the velvety pink couches. Each visit would end in a longing glance at the floaty tulle skirts and fairy wings that were displayed on the wall, glittering temptingly at me.

I remember the lush green of my grandfather’s front yard, and the frustration of watching my older sister, taller, braver, scale that tree with branches too high and terrifying for me to reach (it was hardly more than a sapling).

I remember bringing my clothes to the hallway on freezing winter mornings, letting them warm up on the radiator, then the deliciously cozy feeling of pulling on toasty socks.

I remember sweltering summer afternoons, walking the neverending five blocks home from school. I let my mother do most of the work, tugging me along by the hand as I dragged my feet up 105th street. I remember the thick and soupy air; each step dragged laboriously through the hot porridge of city heat. Climbing the flight of stairs to our second floor apartment seemed an impossible task. Then, kicking off my shoes, I’d race my sister down the long hallway to the living room. There was no better feeling than shoving my perspiring face inches in front of the humming air conditioner. I could hardly breathe with the stream of frigid air blowing straight into my face, so I’d have to take breaks to gasp in a breath before surrendering again to that glacial relief.

I remember the anxiety that I didn’t know how to control. Sitting in chorus for long hours, the terrifying thoughts would nag me, thoughts that something awful would happen to my parents. Eventually, I’d have to ask to go to the bathroom, then go check on them in the waiting room, making sure they were still alive.

I remember walking among the rows of keychains in travel stores, an endless sea of small metallic rectangles and ovals that clinked satisfyingly against each other, each emblazoned with the name of some Emma or Sophia. I remember my disappointment when there wasn’t an “Ariette” keychain (something I quickly got used to).

I remember my obsession with Mary Poppins culminating in climbing up to my sister’s top bunk with an open umbrella and jumping off, a distance that seemed impossibly far. I was so small, and the umbrella so big, that it sort of worked: the umbrella caught the air, and my imagination finished the job – I flew!

The Brooklyn Bridge Last Night

The Brooklyn Bridge last night: twinkling lights, stars in a gray concrete sky, reflected in glass towers that sprung from the ground, silhouetted against the fading sky. The setting sun washed the sky in blues and oranges and reds, melding together above the crowded walkway. Awed tourists, impatient runners, and vendors were all thrown together in a bustling mix. The runners and New Yorkers were easy to spot, always in a rush, irritated with the slow-moving crowds and constant snapping of cameras.

Although I’m a native New Yorker myself, it wasn’t difficult to see the glittering skyline through a rose-tinted lens. The last time I walked across the Brooklyn bridge, I was a child on an elementary school field trip, and now, walking towards the lights of Manhattan, the borough I was born and raised in, I began to feel that familiar ache that New York City at night always seems to stir up, homesick for a childhood that I left behind years ago.

The perspective of a tourist – wide eyed, attentive, in awe of every detail – isn’t so different to that of a child: when the world is still new, and each aspect of it, no matter how small, is enough to excite. Details you now overlook haven’t yet been lost to the mundanity of everyday life. Pain hasn’t yet wrapped itself around places or people, and nostalgia’s cruelty hasn’t tainted one’s perspective. I realized that I never pay much attention to the luxury of seeing a place for the first time. So many opportunities to make places your own, associations that have not yet come to be, and may easily be as painful as they may be good.

I made myself slow down as I walked, fighting against the New York urge to constantly rush, and focused on the sights around me: it was not so hard to notice things that I hadn’t before.
